2. Scope

This policy covers two distinct things:

  • The public website (certimens.fr, in both of its language versions, including the agents' installation pages under /plugins/), which is purely informational: it sets no cookies, uses no analytics and collects no data. Its contact form opens your own mail application without recording anything.
  • The certification application (monespace.certimens.fr), which requires an account and where essentially all of the processing described below takes place.

3. Data collected

3.1 User account

Email address, password (never kept in clear text: only a bcrypt hash is stored), role, institution and class, display language, and account creation date. The roles are: standalone account with no institution, student, teacher, institution administrator and platform administrator.

An account comes into being in one of three ways. By invitation from an institution: the account is created without a password and carries a single-use activation token, with an expiry date, until its holder activates it. By self-registration, which creates an account with no institution and no class, confirmed by a verification email. Or on a first sign-in through an external provider — your institution's own, Google, Microsoft, or a launch from your Moodle (see 3.2). In the first two cases, a request concerning an address that already exists gets exactly the same answer as any other: the platform gives no way of finding out whether an address has an account.

An account created through an external provider has no password at all, and needs none: it is the provider that attests to who you are. Conversely, an account that already had a password keeps it: declaring a provider adds a door, it closes none.

The language is stored on the account rather than inferred from your browser, because some emails are sent outside any visit — a password reset triggered by an administrator, for instance. It is the only way to write to the recipient in their own language rather than in the language of whoever triggered the message.

The account may also carry a first name, a last name and a profile picture. All three are optional: an account with none works exactly the same. A picture you upload is resized and re-encoded by the server before being stored — never the bytes we received — which strips the original file's metadata, including the GPS coordinates phones routinely write into it: we therefore do not keep where the picture was taken. Only PNG and JPEG are stored, at most 512 pixels on the longest side and 512 KB.

Within the application, an account's picture is visible to those to whom a listing they were entitled to read presents it: a teacher and the administration see their students and their colleagues, a student sees their teacher.

An institution may fill in the names and pictures of its accounts by importing its roster. An import completes, it never replaces: it only sets a name or a picture on an account that has neither, and the rule is enforced by the server rather than by the interface. Anyone can change or remove theirs at any time from the “My account” page.

The account finally carries two settings: whether notifications are also sent by email (see 3.8) and whether you may be named in full on the public listing (see 3.3) — both changeable at any time, the second off by default. The light or dark theme is not stored on the account: it stays in the browser.

3.2 Authentication data

Signing in produces opaque access tokens, stored as a hash and never in clear text, along with their creation, last-use and expiry dates. A user can review and revoke their tokens at any time from their workspace. A token's raw value is shown once only, when it is created.

Tokens meant for the agent also carry a label, and their expiry is optional: an agent token is created without one, because the agent runs unattended and a token expiring mid-term would silently stop the measurement of an entire class. It therefore stands until it is revoked — from your workspace, or from the agent itself when you sign out of it.

No agent ever asks for your password, and there is none to ask for: an account may have none at all (see 3.1). The agent pairs itself through a short code that it displays and that you confirm in your Certimens workspace — the same mechanism as a television set or a games console (the device authorization grant, RFC 8628). The confirmation screen tells you which agent is asking, and you can refuse; a refusal is recorded, so that a code someone talked you into reading out stops being usable immediately. The code is valid for ten minutes. What is kept on your machine once pairing is done is the token and nothing else: never a password. Software that presents itself as a Certimens agent and asks for a password is not ours.

Signing in through an external provider. Your institution may open sign-in to its own identity provider, or to Google, Microsoft and Facebook, and may let its Moodle bring you straight in from a course (LTI 1.3). What we then keep is the opaque identifier the provider gives us for you, together with that provider's name — not a token, not a password, and nothing that would let us act on your behalf over there. The rest of what it sends — first name, last name, address, language — is used once, to create the account, and becomes ordinary account data that you can correct: nothing is silently resynchronised behind your back. A launch from a Moodle adds the course you came from, which attaches you to a class exactly as an administrative import would have. The secrets of an exchange in progress are single-use and live between one and ten minutes, and no cookie is set along the way (see 10).

3.3 Uploaded documents

The document name, its content type, the content itself, the assignment it belongs to and its public status, if any. A document is only ever transmitted when the student explicitly asks for it; it never leaves the machine during measurement. A document is visible only to its author, to the teachers and administrators of their institution once it has been submitted, and to the platform; it becomes more widely accessible only if its author explicitly makes it public — and the way you are named there is described just below.

On that public listing, which needs no account at all to read, you are named “Marie D.” — your first name and the initial of your last — and your picture is not shown. That is the default, and what an account that never opened its settings holds, including an account created by an administrator's import. If you gave no name, nothing about you is published: least of all your email address. You can choose to be named in full, with your picture, from the “My account” page; that choice applies only to the documents you make public, and it can be reversed at any time.

Only the formats of a written assignment are accepted: Word (.docx), OpenDocument (.odt), PDF, plain text (.txt, .md) and source files, for an assignment handed in as code. The format is recognised from the file's contents, never from its name; anything else — image, video, archive, executable — is refused before a single byte is stored.

On upload, the document's character count is measured server-side. That figure is used to cross-check the volume the agent reports (see 3.5) and involves keeping no text at all: it is a number.

3.4 Typing metrics

This is the heart of the service. The Certimens agent — a browser extension for Google Docs, Word Online and Overleaf, an Office add-in for Word, an extension for LibreOffice Writer, or an extension for Visual Studio Code, which measures a code project file by file — measures aggregated numerical counters over time windows:

  • the actual size of the document and the total number of keystrokes;
  • effective typing time and the number of characters inserted or pasted;
  • immediate corrections, delayed rewordings, wholesale revisions;
  • navigation jumps within the document;
  • cognitive pauses — resuming after between three seconds and five minutes of inactivity;
  • the regularity of the typing rhythm (median absolute deviation of the intervals between keystrokes) and, depending on the agent, median cadence, number of pastes and number of times the document was left: each one sends only what its editor actually lets it observe.

A window is closed and sent every 200 keystrokes, after two seconds of inactivity, on save, or when you leave the document; a document produces a few hundred of them. Its period runs from the first to the last activity: idle time never enters it. Measurement can be paused at any time from the agent: nothing is counted then, and the pause lasts until the student lifts it, even after a restart. From these measurements we derive the Cognitive Friction Index, a score out of 100 combining four dimensions: the rhythm of cognitive pauses, the revision rate, the regularity of the typing rhythm and navigation fluidity (see section 6).

A window can be declared offline: with no network, the agent queues its measurements and sends them once the connection comes back, stating that they were produced that way. The certificate then carries the share of keystrokes typed offline. It is a statement, not a penalty: that share does not enter the score, and declaring a window offline removes none of the signals in section 3.5 — a batch arriving late is still flagged as such, whether or not it announces itself as a catch-up.

Beyond the editors it knows, the browser extension can measure a site you name yourself: your institution's Moodle, its wiki, whatever tool an assignment is written in online. Your institution may propose a list of such addresses, which the extension shows in its options; proposing is not granting — it is your browser that asks you for permission, for that one address, and nothing is measured on a site you have not accepted. The page's address identifies the document and does not leave your machine: only its title is sent. A permission can be withdrawn at any time from the extension's options.

One local memory, and only one. To tell a paragraph you move around your own document from text imported from elsewhere, the agent remembers what leaves the document — what you copy or cut — as numerical fingerprints of four consecutive words, never the text. At most five excerpts, in memory, erased after ten minutes without use or when the document is closed. Nothing is written to disk, nothing enters a measurement window, nothing is sent: only the counter that comes out of it crosses the network, and a 32-bit fingerprint does not give its words back.

And with nothing installed at all. The application carries an editor of its own, reachable from your workspace: the document is written there in your browser, in Word format, and the editor itself does the measuring. The counters are the ones above, computed by the same rules — an assignment written there and one written in Word are compared on equal terms. The document record is created at the first measurement rather than when the page opens: until you click to save it, it exists with its measurements and no text at all. Nothing is kept in your browser from one session to the next.

What we do not collect: no sequence of keystrokes is recorded, no typed text is captured by the agent, no screenshots, no access to the webcam or microphone, no browser lockdown or monitoring. The measurements are numbers — for instance “42 cognitive pauses between 2.03 p.m. and 2.18 p.m.” — and cannot be used to reconstruct what was written.

You do not have to take our word for it — for the agents. Their source code is public: you can read what is measured, what is sent and what is not, at github.com/Certimens/plugins. The built-in editor, by contrast, runs in a page our servers send you and its code is not published: what it measures and what it sends are the same, but on that particular point you are taking our word for it. We would rather say so than stretch to the editor a guarantee that only covers the agents.

3.5 Integrity checks

Measurement runs on the student's own machine, so it is cross-checked server-side; without that it would be worth nothing. Three things follow from it, all derived from data already described:

  • the share of the document that no measurement accounts for, obtained by comparing the server-side count with the volume reported by the agent. Above 20%, the interface shows a warning, to the document's owner as well as to the teacher. It is also the only element in this section that bears on the score: the points earned are multiplied by the share actually typed, without which a text pasted four fifths of the way through could show full marks next to the warning contradicting it;
  • the shape of the session: how many measurement windows there were and the span they cover, both carried on the certificate. “Measured in 3 windows, from 2.02 p.m. to 2.09 p.m.” reads very differently from “in 40 windows over two days”;
  • technical signals about how measurements arrive. Every batch is examined afterwards and anything that does not look like the agent is noted on the document: headers the browser or the HTTP library adds on its own, the declared content type, the shape of the request body, a batch arriving long after the period it describes, a clock running ahead. These signals are recorded per document as counters — the kind of signal, how many times it occurred, its first and last date — and carry no content from the document.

These signals do not enter the score: they say how the measurements arrived, not how the text was written. They are shown to the marker and not displayed to the author, so as not to tell anyone minded to work around them which ones are in place — which does not place them beyond your right of access (see section 13).

3.6 Access log

Every reading of a document or of a certificate, and every use by a platform administrator of the “view as” feature, is written to an append-only log: the date, the account under whose authority the action was taken, the administrator when they were acting as someone else, the nature of the action and the identifier of the object concerned. No content appears in it — an actor, a subject, an action: the log does not become a second copy of what it watches.

This log is kept for three years, and it outlives what it describes — the document as much as the deletion of the account concerned. That is deliberate: the question “who looked at this document?” arises precisely when a student challenges a result, often once everything else has been erased. Three years is the document's own retention year plus two full years of challenge. It contains no document, no measurement and no email address: only internal identifiers and dates.

The sweep can only delete on age: never on an actor, a subject or an action. Nobody can therefore make an inconvenient event disappear; one can only make everyone grow older together.

Two other actions are recorded there, besides the impersonation already mentioned: moving an assignment's deadline — the one act that reopens an assignment to new submissions — and every failed sign-in. A failure has no known author, by definition: it is recorded against the account aimed at when the address is one of ours, and against nothing at all when it is not, so that the log does not become the list of addresses somebody tried. The counter that slows attempts down (see section 9) is likewise keyed by a hash of the address, never by the address itself.

3.7 Technical data

IP address and connection logs, used to keep the service secure and to limit the number of requests per address: 300 requests a minute across the API, 15 on the authentication routes and 5 on the help form of the sign-in page. This limiting protects against brute-force attacks and automated scraping.

A log line carries: the date, the method and route called, the response code, the latency, the IP address, a request identifier and, for an authenticated caller, the internal identifier of their account — never their email address, never the content of a document nor the detail of a measurement. These logs are readable only by a platform administrator, from an internal screen, and are sent to no third-party collection service.

3.8 Notifications

The application keeps, for each account, the list of what it needs to know: work submitted, assignment published, class or institution joined, account invited, password changed, access token created. Each notification carries its kind, its date, the object to open and a copy of the few details its wording needs, taken at that moment; reading one timestamps it rather than erasing it. These notifications are addressed: nobody but their recipient reads them, not even a platform administrator, and deleting the account takes them with it.

They are also sent by email, unless you switch that copy off from the “My account” page: the bell keeps working, only the sending stops. Two notices escape that setting: a password change and the creation of an access token. Somebody whose password has just been changed without their knowledge must be able to learn it somewhere other than an application they can no longer sign in to.

6. The automated score, and what it is not

The Cognitive Friction Index is computed automatically, by adding four sub-scores — the rhythm of cognitive pauses (30 points), the revision rate (30), the regularity of the typing rhythm (25) and navigation fluidity (15) — whose total is then multiplied by the share of the document actually typed. A level is derived from it by a simple threshold. That is automated processing, and you are entitled to know what it produces.

Each sub-score is continuous: it is interpolated between reference values rather than stepping between fixed ones, so that a hair's difference in behaviour cannot abruptly cost several points. And below 200 keystrokes over the whole document, the platform returns no verdict at all: a three-line exercise has nothing to rework, and must not be mistaken for a fabricated text.

It produces no decision. No mark, no penalty and no report is issued by the platform: the index is one element of judgement handed to the teacher, who decides alone and alongside everything else they know about their student. Certimens is not told what follows, and imposes nothing.

The index describes how a text was written. It does not, on its own, constitute proof of cheating, and it must not be presented as such: it is an indicator that opens a conversation. That is precisely why the certificate sets out the detail — each of the four sub-scores, the raw figures they come from, the number of windows, the period covered and the share of the document that no measurement accounts for — so that a result can be discussed rather than simply imposed. A dimension the sensor could not observe scores nothing and is taken out of the maximum, which the certificate states.

You may ask for human intervention, put your point of view and contest the result: with your institution in the first instance, and with us at contact@certimens.fr.

7. Retention periods

  • Account and academic data: kept for the duration of the relationship with the institution, then deleted.
  • Documents and metrics: destroyed one year after the document was uploaded. An automated task runs every hour: it deletes the stored document, then all of the per-window measurements attached to it, and marks the record as archived. The operation is irreversible.
  • What survives that destruction: the document's record and its certificate — the score, the per-type totals and the shape of the session. The certificate therefore remains readable years later, but the document itself can no longer be downloaded and the per-window detail no longer exists.
  • Authentication tokens: deleted on sign-out, on revocation or on expiry. A token created without an expiry lives until it is revoked or the account is deleted.
  • Access log: kept for three years, beyond both the document and the account it relates to, for the reasons set out in section 3.6.
  • Notifications: kept for one year, because each one carries a frozen copy of what it announces — a document's name, a student's address — taken at the moment of the event. Deleting the account takes them with it.
  • Failed sign-ins and sending quotas: a few hours. A failure is indexed by a hash of the address, never by the address itself.
  • Technical logs: not archived. The server keeps its most recent lines in an in-memory buffer, lost on every restart, and writes a copy to disk that rotates by size and is kept for two generations per instance; the files of an instance that has stopped are deleted twelve hours after its last line. Retention is therefore a volume rather than a duration — the oldest lines disappear as new ones are written — and never exceeds twelve months.

Deleting an account deletes its tokens, its documents — including the files themselves in storage, which are erased before the account —, the associated metrics and its notifications.

8. Recipients and processors

Your data is never sold, rented or transferred to third parties for commercial purposes. Within the application, access is strictly governed by role: a teacher only sees the work submitted by their own classes, an administrator only their own institution.

Our only technical processors are:

  • OVH SAS — hosting of the application server, the database and document storage, as well as sending transactional emails (invitations, password resets).
  • GitHub, Inc. — hosting of the static public website, which receives no application data.

Uploaded documents and typing metrics stay on our own infrastructure: file storage is a service we run ourselves, on the same server as the application, and not a third-party storage service. No data is sent to any analytics service.

9. Security

  • Communications encrypted end to end with TLS (HTTPS).
  • Passwords stored as a bcrypt hash, access tokens as a SHA-256 hash.
  • Role-based access control applied to every request; the “view as” feature is ignored for any account that is not a platform administrator, and logged whenever it is used.
  • Rate limiting per IP address, tightened on authentication routes.
  • Sign-in attempts slowed progressively: five consecutive failures are free, then each failure imposes a wait that doubles, from 15 seconds to 15 minutes at most — a ceiling, because an unlimited lockout would itself be a way of blocking an account. The counter is keyed by a hash of the address, never by the address.
  • Profile pictures decoded, resized and re-encoded by the server: the bytes received are neither stored nor served, and the type an upload declares is never taken at face value.
  • File formats restricted, and recognised from the contents, never from the name.
  • Services run without administrative privileges, with regular backups.

10. Cookies and local storage

No cookie is set, neither on the public website nor in the application. No consent banner is therefore required.

The application keeps your session token in your browser's local storage, so that you do not have to sign in again on every page. That storage is strictly necessary for the service to work and is cleared when you sign out.

11. Transfers outside the European Union

Application data is hosted in France. Visiting the public website involves a United States company (GitHub, Inc., for hosting the pages), on the basis of the EU–US Data Privacy Framework. No certification data is concerned, and the public website loads no third-party resources.

A second case depends on your institution: if it opens sign-in to Google, Microsoft or Facebook (see 3.2), signing in through one of them goes through that company, and all three are United States companies — also on the basis of the Data Privacy Framework. It is a transfer you trigger by choosing that button: the other doors in, including your institution's own identity provider and its Moodle, do not go through them, and no certification data is ever sent to an identity provider.

12. Your rights

Under the GDPR and the French Data Protection Act, you have the following rights:

  • Right of access: to confirm that your data is being processed and to obtain a copy of it.
  • Right to rectification: to have inaccurate or incomplete data corrected.
  • Right to erasure: to request the deletion of your data.
  • Right to restriction: to request that its use be temporarily frozen.
  • Right to object: to object to processing on grounds relating to your particular situation.
  • Right to portability: to retrieve the data you provided to us in a structured format.
  • Human intervention: to have a person review a result, and to contest it (see section 6).

If you are a student or a teacher at a client institution, your institution is your first point of contact; we act as a processor on its behalf.

13. Exercising your rights

Two of them can be exercised directly from your workspace, without writing to us and without waiting:

  • Export your data hands you, as a single JSON file, everything the platform holds about you: your account and each of your documents with its figures. That is the right of access and the right to portability.
  • Delete your account erases the account, its documents — stored files included — and their measurements. It takes effect immediately and cannot be undone. Only platform administrators are excluded: their account has to be deleted by another administrator, so that a platform cannot be left with none.

You can also delete a single document at any time from your Documents area, without waiting for the one-year deadline: the record, the stored file and the associated measurements go together.

For everything else, write to us at contact@certimens.fr, stating what your request concerns. If, after contacting us, you consider that your rights have not been respected, you may lodge a complaint with the French data protection authority (CNIL) at cnil.fr.
